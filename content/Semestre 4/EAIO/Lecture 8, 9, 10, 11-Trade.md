## International Trade
### MDGs and SDGs
In the MDGs framework trade was confined to Goal 8 (develop a global partnership for development) and mainly referred to as a matter of market access and tariff reduction.
- 8.A: developing *an open, rule-based, predictable, nondiscriminatory trading and financial system*.
- target 8.B: providing LDCs with enhanced market access to their exports, via tariff-free and quota-free market access, with a view to addressing their special needs (target 8.B).
Both targets may be considered unfinished business of the Millennium Development Goals. The rationale for developing *further an open, rule-based, predictable, non-discriminatory trading system* was not explicit except that of promoting trade.

In the context of the post-2015 development agenda, international trade is presented as an *enabler* for achieving a broad range of development goals through promoting inclusive and sustainable economic growth.
- When properly harnessed, the opportunities brought by international trade can be a powerful force for creating jobs, enabling efficient use of resources, providing incentives to entrepreneurs and ultimately improving standards of living in all countries.
	- Trade can be good, only under certain circumstances. There is no framework to discover exactly which but basically winners and losers need to be reconciled.

### Terms of trade
- The terms of trade or "net barter" terms of trade is the ratio of the export unit value index to the import unit value index.
- As a simple example, if a country imports product X and exports product Y, and the price of X increases and that of Y decreases, then their terms of trade worsen over time, as for the same amount of exports it can purchase less imports.
- The purchasing power index of exports, or “income terms of trade” is the value index of exports deflated by the import unit value index.
	- Better measure.
	- Prices can decrease but power might not, not necessarily.
- This indicator is an alternative or complement to the net barter terms of trade: if a country increases its exports significantly, this can sometimes negatively affect the price of those exports, and thus while its net barter terms of trade worsen, increased value from exports improves its income terms of trade vis-à- vis its imports, which is what this indicator measures.
- Exporting allows/helps to import, an incentive to trade beyond the returns of exports.
	- You can also do it using loans but that's not the best approach, risky.
- If you export a lot because of your efficiency you may reduce price (supply & demand laws) but you may also sell more.

#### Forms of tariffs
- Regardless of whether a tariff is bound or applied on preferential versus non- discriminatory basis, the tariff can take several forms.
- The most common is an ad valorem tariff, which means that the customs duty is calculated as a percentage of the value of the product.
- Many countries’ tariff schedules also include a variety of non ad valorem tariffs.
- Specific tariffs are computed on the physical quantity of the good being imported.
	- e.g., Australia’s 2005 schedule includes a tariff of $1.22/kg on certain types of cheeses and the United States charges $0.68 per live goat..
- The physical quantity may be expressed in ways that are difficult to determine without laboratory equipment.
	- The European Union charges duties on certain dairy products based on the weight of lactic matter in the product, and the United States charges a tariff on raw cane sugar that varies with the sucrose content of sugar: “1.4606 cents/kg less 0.020668 cents/kg for each degree under 100 degrees (and fractions of a degree in proportion) but not less than 0.943854 cents/kg.”.
![[Pasted image 20260427234201.png]]
Developed countries use it more as you need good institutions and customs to actually carry out these computations on the import of goods.

- Import tariffs are not the only policy measures affecting international commodity trade.
- Over the course of the past century, governments have expanded regulatory controls over economic activity, often in pursuit of social, public health, environmental, or other non-economic policy objectives.
	- Like public or private standards (**e.g**: ISO. Private can often become public though)
- As countries increasingly reduce tariff rates, these non- tariff measures can become more important.
 - It refers to any policy that interferes with exports or imports other than a simple tariff.
	- NTB - Barriers (negotiated away).
	- NTM - Measures (may be legitimate, some standards).
- NTM: Policy measures, other than ordinary customs tariffs, that can ==potentially== have an economic effect on international trade in goods, changing quantities traded, or prices or both.

	- Technical measures most important in the governance of global trade, doesn't have blocking of trade as primary task->like maximum amount of pesticides in exported products or rules of origin like the US has tried to apply (2/3 of value addition in your country if you wanna export or no preferential treatment).

### Subsidies
- Increase domestic production.
- Lower world prices.
- Cause distortions both on imports and exports.
- Subsidies vs border protection:
	- A 10 percent tariff will always have a larger impact on world prices than a subsidy that provides an equivalent level of support.
	- The reason is twofold: a) subsidies do not affect domestic consumption, while tariffs do by increasing consumer prices; and b) a larger share of subsidies does not directly affect prices as they are decoupled from production.
#### WTO
- Agricultural subsidies are grouped in one of three boxes (green, blue or amber) at the WTO, depending on the degree to which they distort trade:
	- Green box subsidies are permitted because they are deemed to cause minimal distortion, and typically include those to research and development (R&D), environmental protection and animal welfare.
	- Blue box subsidies are also permitted, but on the condition that they must not lead to increased production.
	- We basically try to let farmers have a decent income.
- All domestic support considered to distort production and trade (with some exceptions) falls into the amber box, which is defined in Article 6 of the Agreement on Agriculture as all forms of domestic support except that placed in the blue and green boxes.
	- Included in the amber box are measures to support prices, and subsidies directly related to production quantities.
	- Amber-box support is subject to limits expressed in terms of a "Total Aggregate Measurement of Support" (Total AMS) which combines all supports for specified products, together with supports that are not for specific products, into one single figure.

### Effects of tariffs
- A tariff (imposed by country A on imports from country B) creates a wedge between the price received by the foreign producers (country B) and the price paid by domestic consumers (country A).
- The tariff generates revenues: ad valorem tariff: tariff X imported value; specific tariff (on quantity): tariff X quantity; any combination of the two.
- Why imposing a tariff? promote domestic infant industries; protect domestic industries from foreign competitors (legal by WTO rules if unfair foreign competition or immediate competitive threat); raise fiscal revenues; reduce trade deficits; take advantage of an influent demand on international markets (terms of trade improvement): Welfare improving? No, not justified from a welfare point of view.
	- Tax stuff a major concern for developing countries, they want the fiscal revenue.
![[Pasted image 20260513185555.png]]
Allows domestic firms to be profitable as it brings the international price closer to the domestic one, firms that are foreign and do not have a low price could make negative profit and thus lose market access. Lowers imports as we can see. Domestic producers sell more.
![[Pasted image 20260513190144.png]]
Here more consumers buy as prices are lower but producers sell less. There are tensions when it comes to who loses/wins, they want different things. Reality is more difficult though, some consumers are producers. Consumers see net welfare gains from free trade.

Fiscal revenues are generated but we also see a net welfare loss. Consumers pay more for the same good. Consumers and suppliers constantly react, this is inevitable... we create an artificial wedge. Maybe with some inelastic behaviour we could reduce it, but never get to perfection.

#### Possible scenarios
- Total trade effect = trade creation + trade diversion.
- Trade creation: effect of absolute change in price of imports due to tariff.
- Trade diversion: effect of change in relative prices (additional trade creation).
- Trade deficit: Exports-Imports.
- Tariffs affect negatively imports: reduction in trade deficit; However, if retaliation by trade partners: reduction of exports : uncertain effect on trade deficit.
	- Also, cost of inputs can increase. This leads to higher production costs for exports (produce less and/or sell less).
	- In a static and theoretical world where countries only export final goods maybe this would be a good idea, alas... we don't live there.
- Trade deficit = net inflow of goods and services : necessarily matched by net inflow of capital.
	- If you aren't attractive enough to get inflow of capital you can't compensate for trade deficit. If you can't keep up your debt you devalue your currency... Basically if there isn't this match, the deficit is not sustainable.
