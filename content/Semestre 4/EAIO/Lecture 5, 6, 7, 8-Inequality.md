#### Evolution summarised globally
- Measures of inequality based on Gini coefficients of gross and net incomes have increased substantially since 1990 in most of the developed world.
- Inequality, on average, has remained stable in DVGs, albeit at a much higher level than that observed in advanced economies.
- However, there are large disparities across DVGs, with Asia and Eastern Europe experiencing marked increases in inequality, and countries in Latin America exhibiting notable declines (although the region remains the most unequal in the world).

### Measurement : intro

- Net household wealth: sum of financial assets (e.g. equity or bonds) and non-financial assets (e.g. housing or land) owned by individuals, net of their debts.
- Inequality of opportunities is often measured by tracking health, education and human development outcomes by income group, or by examining access to basic services and opportunities.

#### Gini
- A popular measure of inequality is the Gini coefficient, which ranges from 0 (perfect equality) to 1 (perfect inequality) but is typically in the range of 0.3-0.5 for per capita expenditures.
- The Gini is derived from the Lorenz curve, which sorts the population from poorest to richest, and shows the cumulative proportion of the population on the horizontal axis and the cumulative proportion of expenditure (or income) on the vertical axis.
- While the Gini coefficient has many desirable properties – mean independence, population size independence, symmetry, and Pigou-Dalton Transfer sensitivity – it cannot easily be decomposed to show the sources of inequality.
- Can be used therefore to trace the impact of redistributive policies.

##### International Ginis
- Unweighted inter-country inequality is calculated across GDPs obtained from household surveys of all countries in the world, without population-weighting.
- The population-weighted inter-country inequality takes into account population weights.
- Finally, the global inequality concept focuses on individuals, instead of countries. The calculation is based on household surveys with data on individual incomes or consumption.
#### Theil's index
- There are a number of measures of inequality that satisfy criteria allowing for the decomposition of inequality into the part that is due to inequality within areas (e.g. urban, rural) and the part that is due to differences between areas (e.g. the rural- urban income gap).
- The most widely used are the Theil indexes Theil’s T and Theil’s L.
- In most cases at least three-quarters of inequality in a country is due to within-group inequality, and the remaining quarter to between-group differences.
- Can help see what's behind inequality and its changes.

#### Limitations
- One problem with measuring income inequality with the Theil index or Gini coefficient, or with relative income per capita growth between poor and rich individuals/countries is that it summarises changes in the entire income distribution into one number.
	- For example, a decline in income inequality can be perfectly consistent with poor individuals becoming poorer if there is more equality between high and middle-income individuals.
	- Or, a country may experience both a Gini-reducing decrease in poverty and a rise in the share of income going to the top 10%, which increases the Gini; if these effects offset each other, the overall Gini can remain constant, creating the impression that the distribution of income is not changing—while in fact the middle class is being squeezed out.
	- Transfer policy assessment or policy assessment in general can be impacted by this. Niches can be created for the rich, the poorest, etc.
- The value of the Gini index is not necessarily easy to interpret: above which value inequality can be seen as non-acceptable?
	→Moral judgement
- It's like GDP/capita which doesn't give distribution info, here we get closer but this is far from perfect.
##### Other indicators
An alternative way of looking at this is to explore changes along the entire income distribution:
- Average income/wealth per quantile group.
- Growth rates per quantile groups.
	- Indicator 10.1.1 of the SDGs: Growth rates of household expenditure or income per capita among the bottom 40 per cent of the population and the total population.
- Share of national income captured by each quantile group.
- Quantiles ratio
	- The Palma ratio focuses on the differences between those in the top and bottom income brackets: the ratio takes the richest 10% of the population’s share of gross national income (GNI) and divides it by the poorest 40% of the population’s share.
- The simplest measurement of inequality sorts the population from poorest to richest and shows the percentage of expenditure (or income) attributable to each fifth (quintile) or tenth (decile) of the population.
- The poorest quintile typically accounts for 6-10% of all expenditure, the top quintile for 35-50%.
- Decile dispersion ratio: ratio of the average income of the richest 10 percent of the population divided by the average income of the bottom 10 percent.

#### Outcomes and opportunities
- The discourse on inequality makes a distinction between inequality of outcomes (as measured by income, wealth, or expenditure) and inequality of opportunities―attributed to differences in circumstances beyond the individual’s control, such as gender, ethnicity, location of birth, or family background.
- Inequality of outcomes arises from a combination of differences in opportunities and individual’s efforts and talent.
- It is not easy to separate effort from opportunity, especially in an intergenerational context (income or whatever already accumulated by ancestors).
- For instance, parental income, resulting from their own effort, determines the opportunity of their children to obtain an education.
- It is in this spirit that Rawls (1971) argued that the distribution of opportunities and of outcomes are equally important and informative to understand the nature and extent of inequality around the world.
- Traditional economic theory (marginal productivity theory) suggests that incomes reflect individuals’ contributions to society.
	- Not very helpful considering real inequality mechanics, however,
	- Some degree of inequality may not be a problem insofar as it provides the incentives for people to excel, compete, save, and invest to move ahead in life.
	- **For example**, returns to education and differentiation in labor earnings can spur human capital accumulation and economic growth, despite being associated with higher income inequality.
##### But
- However, marginal productivity theory (optimisation is in the margin, we have to pay attention to it) assumes no market failure. With market failures outcomes may not be efficient anymore. We can't see everything from the marginal productivity theory standpoint.
- Inequality may stem from rent-seeking (i.e. earning income not through productivity but by extracting wealth from others.
- Together with some institutional and political factors rent-seeking may help explain the rise in inequality observed since the 1980s (e.g. Stiglitz 2016).
###### **Proof**
1. Corporate Executives: CEO pay has increased exponentially (from 20x the average worker’s wage in 1965 to >300x in 2010s) without a corresponding increase in productivity or firm performance.
2. Financial Sector: Bankers and financiers benefit from implicit government subsidies (e.g., "too big to fail" protections) and anti-competitive practices, leading to excessive pay premiums.
	- **E.g.**: 2008.
3. Monopoly Power & Intellectual Property Rents: Wealth accumulation increasingly comes from control over assets (e.g., land, patents) rather than productive investment.
	- Innovation should be rewarded but not excessively, tough to tell how much though.
	- Bad platforms that you have to use because of lack of aternatives.
4. Decline of Unions: Weakened labor bargaining power due to anti-union policies (e.g., Reagan’s suppression of strikes).
5. Asymmetric Globalisation: Capital can move freely across borders, but labor cannot, suppressing wages.
6. Tax & Regulatory Policies: Favorable treatment of capital gains, deregulation, and weak antitrust enforcement have amplified inequality.

We can thus end up in inefficient market setups very costly to society.
#### Positive relationship between inequality and growth
- Unequal Propensity to Save: Richer individuals save more, leading to higher aggregate savings and investment, which fuels growth (Fisher, 1930; Kaldor, 1955).
- Incentives and Equity-Efficiency Trade-off: Inequality motivates effort and investment in education or entrepreneurship, as higher rewards incentivise risk-taking (Mirrlees, 1971; Rebelo, 1991). Redistribution, while reducing inequality, may distort incentives and lower growth.
#### Negative relationship between inequality and growth
- Political Economy Channel: In democracies, higher inequality may lead to demands for redistribution (via median voter theory), increasing taxes and distorting economic incentives (Alesina and Rodrik, 1994; Persson and Tabellini, 1994).
- Sociopolitical Instability: Inequality can fuel crime, unrest, and conflict, undermining investment and growth (Alesina et al., 1996; Benhabib and Rustichini, 1996). Weak institutions exacerbate these effects.
- Credit Market Imperfections: Poor individuals, lacking collateral, cannot invest in human or physical capital, leading to underinvestment and lower growth (Galor and Zeira, 1993; Banerjee and Newman, 1993).
- Demand-Driven Innovation: Inequality affects innovation by altering market size and price incentives for new products. High inequality may limit demand for mass-market innovations (Foellmi and Zweimüller, 2006).
- In addition to affecting growth drivers, inequality could result in poor public policy choices: It can lead to a backlash against growth-enhancing economic liberalization and fuel protectionist pressures against globalisation and market-oriented reforms.
- Enhanced power by the elite could result in a more limited provision of public goods that boost productivity and growth, and which disproportionately benefit the poor.
- Positive channels often operate in the short run while negative channels manifest over longer periods : empirical investigation needed.
##### Empirical evidence
Empirical analysis (IMF 2015) found that:
- Income inequality (as measured by the Gini coefficient) negatively affects growth and its sustainability: if the income share of the top 20 percent increases by 1 percentage point, GDP growth is actually 0.08 percentage point lower in the following five years, suggesting that the benefits do not trickle down.
- Instead, a similar increase in the income share of the bottom 20 percent (the poor) is associated with 0.38 percentage point higher growth.
However, the empirical literature yields mixed results, influenced by data quality, methodologies, and sample selection.
- Early Cross-Country Studies found a negative relationship between inequality and growth, but these relied on limited data.
	- Doesn't take into account the time aspect (evolution).
- Panel studies often found short-term positive effects, while cross-sectional studies emphasised long-term negatives.
- Can't control everything, size of the population **f.ex**. Data availability doesn't always allow to conduct analysis on every variable that could change the relationship. The more information=the better the results.
###### **Key findings**
- Inequality’s effects vary by development level. Negative effects are more pronounced in poor countries, possibly due to credit constraints.
	- Poorness trap.
- Wealth inequality (e.g., land or human capital) often shows stronger effects than income inequality.
- Indeed we can see that other factors impact this relationship. This could be also more consistent with the Kuznets curve we saw, although this one was as we mentioned only cross-sectional.
- Inequality at the top may boost growth (via savings/incentives), while inequality at the bottom hampers it (Voitchovsky, 2005).
	- So it depends, we want transfers which are best to reduce poverty and inequality but inequality at the top isn't that bad. We could strangle growth if we hit the rich too hard.
- Short-term growth may benefit from inequality, but long-term growth suffers.
- Recent Studies continue to diverge. Some find negative effects (Berg et al., 2018), others positive (El-Shagi and Shao, 2019), and some none at all (Ferreira et al., 2018).
- Methodological differences drive these disparities.
