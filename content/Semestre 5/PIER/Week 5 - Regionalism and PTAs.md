### Characteristics of PTAs

![[PIER_Week_5_Slide_07.png]]

- **FTA:** members retain separate external tariffs. Imports could enter through the lowest-tariff member → need rules of origin, specifying sufficient local value added.
- **Customs union:** common external tariff removes this routing incentive.
- **Common market:** adds free movement of labour and capital.
- **Currency union:** adds a shared currency.

Switzerland–EU: separate external tariffs + free movement = a mixture of FTA and common-market features.

### Coverage

![[PIER_Week_5_Slide_08.png]]

PTAs extend into domestic rules governing firms.

- **Procurement:** access to government tenders.
- **SPS:** sanitary and phytosanitary measures. **TBT:** technical barriers, e.g. production/packaging requirements. Protective standards can also restrict trade.

### Example: CH–Mercosur

![[PIER_Week_5_Slide_09.png]]

Agreement between **EFTA and Mercosur**. Market access can coexist with domestic approval requirements, e.g. opening a bank subsidiary.

Labour/environmental commitments lack binding sanctions and are excluded from the main dispute-settlement mechanism. Distinguish commitments from enforceability.

### Domino Theory

![[PIER_Week_5_Slide_10.png]]

A and B sign a PTA → firms in excluded country C lose competitiveness/market share → C’s exporters lobby for their own agreement → further excluded countries react.

**Diffusion through competition:** foreign agreements change domestic lobbying incentives.

Mexico example: 11% → 6% refers to the **EU share of Mexican imports**. European exporters then pushed for EU–Mexico liberalisation.

### PTA Winners: MNCs

![[PIER_Week_5_Slide_11.png]]

Much trade takes place within multinational production networks: intermediate goods cross borders before final assembly.

- Outcome: U.S. affiliates’ sales back to the U.S., e.g. GM Mexico → U.S.
- **Interaction effect:** the gain from a tariff cut increases with affiliate size.
- For the same 10% tariff cut, small affiliates gain little; larger affiliates gain substantially more. Large MNCs therefore have strong incentives to lobby for PTAs.

### Development Strategy

![[PIER_Week_5_Slide_12.png]]

**Time-consistency problem:** government promises favourable conditions → firm builds an immobile factory → government can then change taxes/rules. Investors anticipate this risk.

PTAs lock in reforms and make investment promises more credible → help developing countries compete for FDI.

### FDI Risks and Solutions

![[PIER_Week_5_Slide_13.png]]

- Need low **host-country tariffs** to import inputs and low **home-country tariffs** to sell the output.
- **Regulatory takings:** ownership remains, but new regulations restrict how the investment can be used.

### Economic Reform

![[PIER_Week_5_Slide_14.png]]

New reforming governments face resistance from established economic elites. A PTA offers those elites access to foreign markets, making reform more acceptable, and locks reforms in against reversal.

Graphs: first dashed line = negotiations begin; second = signature. IPR reforms increase around the agreements.

### European Monetary Integration

![[PIER_Week_5_Slide_18.png]]

Breakdown of Bretton Woods → attempt to preserve exchange-rate stability regionally.

Snake unstable; EMS became more stable as French policy converged toward Germany’s restrictive approach. ECB modelled on the independent Bundesbank.

### Optimum Currency Areas

![[PIER_Week_5_Slide_19.png]]

**Trilemma:** capital mobility + fixed exchange rates → loss of national monetary autonomy.

- **Symmetric shocks:** a common monetary response can suit members.
- **Asymmetric shocks:** members need different responses but cannot adjust monetary policy separately.
- Labour mobility, wage flexibility and fiscal transfers help absorb these differences.

### Is EMU an OCA?

![[PIER_Week_5_Slide_20.png]]

Asymmetric-shock problems were identified in **1992**, before the euro. Several countries identified later became crisis countries.

OCA efficiency alone cannot fully explain monetary integration → political explanations.

### Credibility

![[PIER_Week_5_Slide_22.png]]

Political pressure for expansion creates inflation/credibility problems. Fixing to the Deutsche Mark lets governments **borrow Bundesbank credibility** and constrains monetary policy.

### Convergence of Ideas

![[PIER_Week_5_Slide_23.png]]

Germany’s perceived success + perceived failure of Keynesianism → shift toward monetarist beliefs → restrictive policies. France’s 1981–83 expansionary experiment was followed by a restrictive turn.
#### Institutional vs. ideational mechanisms

![[PIER_Week_5_Classroom_Diagrams.png]]

- **Institutional:** low credibility → monetary policy → inflation. Institutions **moderate** the credibility-to-policy relationship: fixed exchange rates, CBI or EMU weaken the effect of credibility problems on policy. Institutions are an **explanatory variable**.
- **Ideational:** shift in beliefs → policy change → inflation convergence → CBI/EMU. Institutions formalise prior changes; they are an **outcome**.

Same broad outcome, different causal mechanisms. **CBI** = central-bank independence.
