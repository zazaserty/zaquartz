## The International Economic System
### What is PIER?
- **Political**: studies the political battle between the winners and the losers from economic exchange.
- **Economic**: investigates the origins and effects of developments in the international economic system.
- **International**: examines the connections between economics and politics beyond the confines of a single state.
#### PIER connects
- **Politics**: international and domestic.
- **Economics**: international and, in brackets, domestic.
### Globalization
- **Economic**: long-distance flows of goods, capital and services across national borders and continents.
- **Political**: diffusion of government policies across countries through the spread of international norms and/or integration into international institutions.
- **Social**: spread of ideas, information, values and people across countries and continents.

### A Very Short History of Globalization
#### When did globalization begin?
- **Possible dates**: 1492? 1820? 1980?
- **Trade ≠ globalization**: the effect on “ordinary people” is crucial for globalization. Things like the Silk Road don't count, normal people didn't buy silk.
- **Key obstacles**:
  - Freight costs: technology.
  - Taxes and tariffs: policy.
  - Basically, when did they decline in a way that trade affected a large amount of people?
- **Measurement**: convergence of prices in the origin and destination countries. Can ordinary people afford the products?

### Policy vs. technology
Price of coffe suddenly became more expensive between 1780 and 1820 because of the Napoleonic wars (blockades and other obstacles to trade). Then when they ended, the price lowered a lot. The markups converged and globalisation began. It was thus in the 19th century when globalisation happened.

Also trains and refrigerators (for oversea transport of agricultural products) appear among others which accelerates this phenomenon further. Little by little technology starts playing a lower effect and policy a bigger one.

| Period    | Price gaps        | Reason                                      |
| --------- | ----------------- | ------------------------------------------- |
| 1820–1914 | Cut by 81%        | Fewer barriers: 28%; cheaper transport: 72% |
| 1914–1950 | Increased by 100% | More barriers                               |
| 1950–2000 | Cut by 76%        | Fewer barriers: 74%; cheaper transport: 26% |
| 1820–2000 | Cut by 92%        | Policies: 18%; technology: 82%              |
Source: O’Rourke & Williamson 1999.
### First era of globalization
#### 19th-century trade
- **Policy shift**: gradual dismantling of tariffs until the 1870s. From the 1780s onwards Britain tries to experiment with trade liberalisation.
- **Key events**:
  - Napoleonic Wars, 1803–1815.
  - Corn Laws, 1815. Tariffs on agricultural products to Britain
	  - British farmers were very opposed to trade because they couldn't export much. Britain was mostly manufacturing and they weren't competitive (much more land than capital in Germany or France compared to Britain). The workers in the industry did want liberalisation though, they were competitive and didn't want to pay for overpriced agricultural goods.
  - Repeal of the Corn Laws, 1846.
	  - Working class and capital class ally against these laws. Trade increases quite a bit.
  - Cobden–Chevalier Treaty, 1860.
	  - One of the first free-trade treaties, famous and has the first most-favoured nation clause.
  - National unifications, 1860s/1870s.
- **Result**: exports as a percentage of world output peaked in 1913.
#### 19th-century money
- **Gold Standard**: Britain moved to gold; other countries followed; tipping point around 1870 (pretty much everyone joined, since so many followed Britain). Money value linked to precious metals in some countries. Isaac Newton, master of the Mint, overvalued the price of gold which caused the silver coins to disappear and get used more than the gold ones.
- **Features**:
  - Fixed exchange rate, since all the currencies are based on these metals.
	  - You have to make sure though that your interests rate are such that these exchange rates are always fixed. The policies for this require prioritising external stability over internal stability, you pay the price domestically for exchange rate stability (increase unemployment or go through a recession even).
  - External stability and internal adjustment.
  - Free convertibility.
  - International gold flows.
### Interwar collapse
#### Rising protectionism
- **Key events**:
  - Smoot–Hawley tariff, United States, 1930.
	  - Protectionism because of the recession, then other states that weren't into agriculture (what the tariff initially protected) demanded protection and tariffs went up to 41% average.
  - Imperial Preference system, United Kingdom, 1932.
	  - Britain followed.
  - Retaliation by other countries.
	  - Trade collapsed.

#### End of gold
- **Phases**:
  - Hyperinflation in the early 1920s.
  - Return to gold, 1924–1931.
	  - Hoping it'll be the solution.
  - Great Depression, from 1929 onward.
- **Dissolution**:
  - Germany and Great Britain: 1931.
  - United States: 1933.
  - France, the Netherlands and Switzerland: 1936.
  - Why didn't it work? Why did everyone leave little by little? The price of gold was fixed in Britain for several hundred years and then just collapsed? How is this explained?
	  - Politics. Democratisation created an incompatibility, voters didn't want internal adjustment when they were going through a crisis.
### Post-war trade regime
#### GATT system
- **Evolution**: GATT established in 1947; membership increased from 23 to 164; formal organization in 1995.
- **Features**:
  - Non-discrimination.
  - Regulation of trade in goods and services.
  - Intellectual property rights (IPR).
- **WTO functions**:
  - Forum for negotiations.
  - Administration of trade agreements.
  - Trade policy review.
  - Dispute settlement.
#### Negotiation rounds
Little by little they moved from tariffs, which declined, to the new biggest obstacle to trade: non-tariffs instruments (**e.g** standards).

### Bretton Woods
- **Evolution**:
  - United States–United Kingdom negotiations.
  - Bretton Woods Conference, 1944.
  - Collapse from 1973 onward.
- **Features**:
  - Fixed but adjustable rates.
  - US dollar linked to gold.
  - Capital controls.
- **IMF**: formal organization created to oversee and coordinate monetary policies and manage crises.
	- Originally Keynesian idea not so neoliberal, contrary to current perceptions.
### End of the millennium
- **Preferential trade**: GATT stagnation; regional trade blocks such as the EU, NAFTA and Mercosur; preferential trade agreements from the 1990s onward.
	- Doha round never finished, countries switched away from multileral GATT to preferential trade agreements.
- **Monetary “non-system”**: spread of floating exchange rates from 1973 onward; variety of exchange-rate regimes; Eurozone.
- **Capital mobility**: dismantling of capital controls; rise of foreign investment.
### Conclusion
#### The “End of History”?
- **1990s thesis**: history has ended with the global dominance of democratic capitalism.
- **Victory of liberalism**: future conflicts will occur within, not against, the liberal democratic model.
- **International Relations**: increasingly focused on economic cooperation rather than military competition.
#### Does history repeat itself?
- **“End of History” postponed**.
- **Political challenges**: rising populism challenges liberal democracy and international cooperation.
- **Economic challenges**: rising protectionism challenges globalization.
